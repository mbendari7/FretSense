## Read

A *scale* is a set of notes in order, starting from a note called the *root* (or *tonic*). The
**major scale** is the one most other scales and chords are measured against, so it is worth
knowing well.

### The step pattern

Every major scale uses the same pattern of whole steps (W, two frets) and half steps (H, one
fret):

**W W H W W W H**

Start on any note, follow the pattern, and you get that note's major scale. It always has seven
different notes, one of each letter, and the eighth note is the root again, an octave higher.

C major is the scale with no sharps or flats: C D E F G A B C. Its half steps fall on E–F and
B–C, exactly where the musical alphabet already has its half steps.

### Building G major

Start on G and follow the pattern:

| Step | W | W | H | W | W | W | H |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| From | G | A | B | C | D | E | F# |
| To | A | B | C | D | E | F# | G |

After E, a whole step up lands on the fret between F and G. The scale has already used E and
still needs an F of some kind, so that note is written F#, not Gb. G major has one sharp: F#.

Do the same from F and you get F G A Bb C D E: one flat, Bb.

### On one string

On a single string the pattern is a fret map. From any starting fret, move up 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2
and 1 frets. From an open string that gives frets 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 12.

### Scale degrees

The notes of a scale are numbered from the root: 1 is the root, 2 the next note, up to 7. In G
major, 1 is G, 3 is B and 5 is D. Those numbers are called *degrees*, and they are how chords are
built from a scale, which the triads lesson covers next.

### What a key is

When a song is "in G major", most of its notes come from the G major scale, and G feels like
home. G is the home note and the G major scale supplies most of the notes: together they make the *key* of G major. Knowing the key tells you which notes are likely to fit.
