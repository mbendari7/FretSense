## Read

An **arpeggio** is a chord played one note at a time instead of all at once. For a bassist, it
is the next step after root, fifth and octave: adding the 3rd tells the listener whether the chord
is major or minor. Guitarists use the same arpeggios for fills and solos that follow the chords.

### One octave up from the root

| Chord | Arpeggio, one octave up | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Major | R, 3, 5, octave | C: C E G C |
| Minor | R, b3, 5, octave | Am: A C E A |
| Dominant 7th | R, 3, 5, b7, octave | G7: G B D F G |
| Major 7th | R, 3, 5, 7, octave | Cmaj7: C E G B C |
| Minor 7th | R, b3, 5, b7, octave | Dm7: D F A C D |

The shapes come straight from the interval lessons: the 3rd and 5th shapes from the root, the
octave shape on top, and the 7th one or two frets below the octave.

### Following the changes

A *chord change* is the moment the music moves to the next chord. Take this progression in
C major, four beats each:

**C – Am – F – G**

The arpeggios are:

- C: C E G
- Am: A C E
- F: F A C
- G: G B D

Look at what neighbouring chords share. C and Am have C and E in common. Am and F share A and C.
Shared notes, called *common tones*, are smooth places to move from one chord to the next.

### A practice routine

1. Play each arpeggio up from its root, one note per beat, four beats per chord (R, 3, 5,
   octave).
2. Loop the progression slowly. Land on the new chord's root right on the change.
3. Once that feels easy, try going down from the octave instead, or start a chord on its 3rd.

The drills ask for one chord at a time and check each note in order. Drill them, then chain them
yourself over the progression above.

## Notes for See

The board shows C major with degree labels. Pick an R low on the neck and trace R, 3, 5 and the
next R upward within a few frets. That is the C arpeggio you will start the progression with.
