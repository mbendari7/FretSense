## Read

The fastest way to learn the neck is to start with two strings and know them cold. A common starting point is the two lowest-sounding strings, because many chord shapes and bass lines have their root there.

This lesson assumes *standard tuning*. On a six-string guitar that is E A D G B E from the
lowest string to the highest. On a four-string bass it is E A D G, the same note names as the
guitar's lowest four strings, one octave lower. If you play in another tuning, the method below
still works: start from your open string's note instead (if your open string is a sharp or flat, come back to this after the sharps and flats lesson).

FretSense numbers strings from the highest-pitched one, which is string 1. So on a six-string
guitar the low E string is string 6; on a four-string bass it is string 4.

### The E string

Start from the open string (fret 0) and use the gaps from the musical alphabet: one fret for E–F
and B–C, two frets for every other pair.

| Fret | 0 | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Note | E | F | G | A | B | C | D | E |

### The A string

| Fret | 0 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Note | A | B | C | D | E | F | G | A |

At fret 12 each string is back to its open note, one octave higher. Above fret 12 the pattern
repeats: fret 13 has the same note name as fret 1, and so on.

### Handy landmarks

- Many necks have position dots at frets 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12. On the E string those dots land on
  G, A, B, C sharp and E; C sharp is the one you will meet in the next lesson. Use the dots at 3,
  5 and 7 as anchors: G, A and B on the E string; C, D and E on the A string.
- Fret 5 on the low E string is A, the same pitch as the open A string. That is a quick way to tune
  the A string by ear.

### How to practise

Say the note name out loud as you play it. Go up the string, then down. Then pick random notes
and find them on both strings. A few minutes a day beats one long session.
