## Read

A bassist's main jobs are usually to make the chord changes clear and keep the groove solid. Three notes do
most of that work: the **root** of the chord, its **perfect 5th**, and the **octave** (the root
again, 12 half steps higher).

These three notes fit under both major and minor chords, because both contain the same perfect
5th. Only the 3rd differs, and this pattern leaves it out. (They don't fit diminished or augmented
chords, whose 5th is a half step lower or higher.)

### The shape in standard tuning

On a bass in standard tuning, each string is a perfect fourth (5 half steps) above the one below
it. That gives one shape you can move anywhere:

- **Root:** any note on any string that has two strings above it.
- **5th:** next string up, two frets higher.
- **Octave:** two strings up, also two frets higher than the root, on the same fret as the 5th.

The whole shape spans three frets, so one hand position covers it.

Example on a four-string bass: A on the E string at fret 5. The 5th, E, is on the A string at
fret 7. The octave, A, is on the D string at fret 7.

### The 5th below

There is a second 5th close by. The note on the *same fret*, one string lower, is a perfect
fourth below the root. Its letter is the chord's 5th, one octave lower. For a D on the A string
at fret 5, the note on the E string at fret 5 is A, the 5th of D. Many bass lines bounce between
the root and this lower 5th.

### Turning it into a bass line

A simple pattern, one note per beat: root, 5th, octave, 5th. Play it four beats per chord, then
move the whole shape so the root matches the next chord. Keep the rhythm steady before you try to
speed it up.

In other tunings, or across the strings of an extended-range bass tuned differently, the shape
changes. The intervals stay the same: the 5th is 7 half steps above the root, the octave 12.
