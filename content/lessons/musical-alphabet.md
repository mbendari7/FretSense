## Read

Music names its notes with just seven letters: **A B C D E F G**. After G you start again at A.
That short loop is called the *musical alphabet*, and every note you will ever play has one of
these letters in its name.

The seven plain letters are called *natural notes*. There are also notes in between some of them,
which you will meet in the lesson on sharps and flats. Counting those, there are 12 different notes
before the pattern repeats.

### One fret is a half step

On a fretted instrument, moving one fret along a string changes the pitch by the smallest step in the standard Western tuning system. That step is called a **half step** (some books say *semitone*).

Two half steps make a **whole step** (or *tone*). On one string, a whole step is a jump of two
frets.

| Move on one string | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 fret | half step |
| 2 frets | whole step |
| 12 frets | the same letter again, higher (an octave) |

### Where the gaps are

Most neighbouring letters are a whole step apart, so there is one fret between them. Two pairs
are different: **E to F** and **B to C** are only a half step apart. There is no note between them.

So, walking up one string through the letters from A, the fret gaps are:

- A to B: 2 frets
- B to C: 1 fret
- C to D: 2 frets
- D to E: 2 frets
- E to F: 1 fret
- F to G: 2 frets
- G to A: 2 frets

Those gaps add up to 12 frets, which brings you back to A.

### Why this matters

If you know one note on a string, you can work out the rest by counting. Remember "E–F and B–C
are neighbours" and you have the whole map.

Try it now: pick any string in standard tuning (every open string there is a natural note), find its letter, and count up the letters using the
gaps above. Check yourself in the See stage.

## Notes for See

The fretboard shows only the natural notes (the C major scale uses all seven letters and nothing
else). Look for places where two labels sit on neighbouring frets of the same string: those are always E–F or B–C.
