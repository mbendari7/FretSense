## Read

Three intervals are the building blocks of major and minor triads: the **minor 3rd**, the **major 3rd** and
the **perfect 5th**. Learn their shapes and chords stop being mysteries.

An interval's name has two parts. The number counts letter names: from C up to E is a 3rd
(C, D, E is three letters), and from C up to G is a 5th. The quality (*major*, *minor*,
*perfect*) tells you the exact size in half steps.

| Interval | Short name | Half steps | Example from C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minor 3rd | m3 | 3 | C to Eb |
| Major 3rd | M3 | 4 | C to E |
| Perfect 5th | P5 | 7 | C to G |

### On one string

Counting frets is always correct, whatever your tuning: a minor 3rd is 3 frets up, a major 3rd is
4 frets up, a perfect 5th is 7 frets up. It is just a long stretch, so players use shapes across
strings instead.

### Across strings in standard tuning

When two neighbouring strings are a perfect fourth apart (5 half steps), which is every pair on a
bass in standard tuning and every guitar pair except G and B, the shapes from a note to the next
higher string are:

- **Perfect 5th:** next string, two frets higher.
- **Major 3rd:** next string, one fret lower.
- **Minor 3rd:** next string, two frets lower.

On a guitar in standard tuning, the G and B strings are a major 3rd apart (4 half steps), so
when you cross from the G string to the B string each shape moves one fret higher:

- Perfect 5th: three frets higher.
- Major 3rd: same fret.
- Minor 3rd: one fret lower.

### A worked example

Take C on the A string, fret 3 (standard tuning).

- G, the perfect 5th, is on the D string at fret 5.
- E, the major 3rd, is on the D string at fret 2.
- Eb, the minor 3rd, is on the D string at fret 1.

### Hearing the difference

Many listeners hear the major 3rd as bright and settled and the minor 3rd as darker. The perfect 5th sounds open and stable; it is the whole of a power chord and a favourite note in bass lines. Play
each one and listen before you move on.
