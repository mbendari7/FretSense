## Read

A **7th chord** is a triad with one more note on top: a 7th above the root. That extra note adds
colour, and it is everywhere in blues, jazz, soul and pop.

The 7th comes in two sizes:

- The **minor 7th** (written b7) is 10 half steps above the root, a whole step below the octave.
- The **major 7th** (written 7) is 11 half steps above the root, a half step below the octave.

### Three common 7th chords

| Chord | Tones | Symbol | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant 7th | root, 3, 5, b7 | 7 | G7: G B D F |
| Major 7th | root, 3, 5, 7 | maj7 | Cmaj7: C E G B |
| Minor 7th | root, b3, 5, b7 | m7 | Am7: A C E G |

Notice the naming. "Dominant 7th" (G7) is a major triad with a minor 7th. "Major 7th" (Cmaj7)
is a major triad with a major 7th. "Minor 7th" (Am7) is a minor triad with a minor 7th.

### Finding the 7th on the neck

Find the root's octave, then step back:

- b7: two frets below the octave.
- 7: one fret below the octave.

That is often easier than counting 10 or 11 frets up.

### 7th chords in a key

Add the 7th from the scale to each triad in C major and you get Cmaj7, Dm7, Em7, Fmaj7, G7, Am7
and Bm7b5. Only the chord on the 5th degree (G7 in C) is a dominant 7th. It has a strong pull
back to the home chord, C, which is why so many songs and blues turnarounds lean on it.

### Listening tip

Play a G major chord, then G7. Many players hear the added F as restless, as if it wants to move. Then play Cmaj7 after C: many hear the added B as softer and dreamier.
